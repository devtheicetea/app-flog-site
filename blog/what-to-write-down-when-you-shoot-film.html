What to write down when you shoot film

A roll of film won't tell you what lens you used, where you stood, or what the light was doing. Here's what to record while you still remember.

Flog · June 2026 · 6 min read

There's a strange gap built into shooting film. You press the shutter today, but you won't see the picture for a week, two weeks, sometimes a month — after the roll is finished, mailed, developed, and scanned. By the time the images land, the moment that made them is gone. You're left squinting at a frame, trying to remember: was that the 50 or the 35? Did I push this roll? Where even was this?

Digital quietly solves this for you. Every file carries EXIF — camera, lens, aperture, shutter, ISO, sometimes GPS. Film carries none of it. The negative is just light and silver; it remembers the scene, not the settings. If you want that information, you have to write it down yourself, in the moment, before it evaporates.

The good news: it takes seconds per frame, and it pays you back every time you look at the scans.

Why bother logging at all

Two reasons, really. The first is learning. Film is a slow feedback loop, and a slow loop only teaches you if you can connect cause to effect. When a frame comes back gorgeous — or muddy, or thin — the log is what tells you why: that you metered for the shadows, that you shot wide open at dusk, that you rated the Tri-X at 1600 and pushed two stops. Without that link, every roll is a fresh roll of the dice.

The second is simply memory. A roll of 36 frames shot across a weekend trip becomes, a month later, 36 small mysteries. A few lines per frame turns them back into a record of where you were and what you saw.

The negative remembers the scene. Only you can remember the settings.

What to log, frame by frame

You don't need all of this every time. But these are the fields worth a column in your notebook — roughly in order of how often you'll thank yourself for them.

  1. 01
    Camera & lens
    Especially if you shoot more than one body or swap glass mid-roll. "Which lens was this?" is the single most common thing photographers wish they'd noted.
  2. 02
    Film stock & rated ISO
    The stock and the speed you actually shot it at — box speed, or pushed/pulled. This drives how the lab develops it, so it matters before the frame even exists as a picture.
  3. 03
    Exposure
    Aperture and shutter speed, and how you metered (incident, spot, sunny-16 by eye). This is the part that turns a good or bad frame into a lesson.
  4. 04
    Where & when
    Location, date, and time. Time of day quietly encodes the light — golden hour, flat noon, blue hour — which explains more about a frame's mood than almost anything else.
  5. 05
    The subject
    One line. "Mara on the fire escape." "Third bridge, looking west." Enough to recognize the frame instantly when the scans come back out of order.
  6. 06
    A reference snap
    A quick phone photo of the same scene. Nothing reconnects a scan to its moment faster than a colour reference you took two seconds later.

And for the whole roll

A handful of things belong to the roll, not the frame: a roll number so you can match it to the canister and, later, to the scans; the dates you loaded and finished it; which lab it went to; and any development notes — push two stops, cross-process, handle gently. Write the roll number on the canister in marker too. Negatives and notebooks get separated; a number on both is how they find each other again.

Notebook, or something faster

A pocket notebook works, and plenty of photographers swear by one. The friction shows up later: a 36-frame roll is a lot of tiny handwriting to do in the cold or the dark, and when the scans arrive you still have to sit down and pair each image to its line by hand. The record and the photos live in two different places, and keeping them married is its own chore.
